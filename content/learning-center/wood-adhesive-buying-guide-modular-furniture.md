For a modular furniture factory or interior contracting firm, **wood adhesive** is a small line on the bill of materials but a big factor in quality, productivity and callbacks. This buying guide sets out how to evaluate, trial and buy wood adhesive at scale — and how to switch suppliers without risking a single project.

## 1. Define your grade requirements

Map every product line to an EN 204 durability class:

| Product line | Recommended class |
|---|---|
| Bedroom wardrobes, beds, TV units | D2 |
| Office furniture, workstations | D2 |
| Kitchen carcasses, shutters, countertops | **D3** |
| Bathroom vanities, utility units | **D3** |
| Coastal / high-humidity projects (all furniture) | **D3** |
| Economy interior joinery | D1 |

Many Kerala contractors now **standardise on D3** for entire projects to avoid mix-ups on site. See [D1–D4 explained](/learning-center/d1-d2-d3-d4-wood-adhesive-classes-en-204-explained).

## 2. Evaluate on cost per square foot, not price per kg

Price per kg is misleading. What matters is:

**Cost per sq ft = (price per kg × kg used per sheet) ÷ sq ft per sheet**

A lower-priced adhesive with lower solids or poor spread can need more kg per sheet — and one delamination callback can wipe out a year of savings. Use our [coverage calculator](/tools/coverage-calculator) to estimate consumption, then compare real usage during trials.

## 3. Check technical fit with your process

- **Application method:** hand spreader, roller, or glue-spreader machine? Viscosity must suit it.
- **Press type:** cold press needs good open time and wet tack; hot press needs fast set and heat stability. See [hot vs cold press](/learning-center/laminate-pasting-adhesive-hot-press-cold-press).
- **Substrates:** plywood, MDF, particle board, pre-lam boards, veneers, PVC/acrylic laminates.
- **Edge banding:** PVAc or hot-melt? Make sure the edge-band and panel adhesives are compatible.

## 4. Assess the supplier, not just the product

Ask:

- Do they **manufacture the resin in-house** or buy it in? In-house resin gives better batch consistency.
- Can they provide a **TDS and MSDS** for each grade?
- Is there **technical support** when something goes wrong?
- What are **lead times** to your location, and is there local stock?
- Are **pack sizes** suitable — small packs for site teams, drums for the factory?
- Will they support **training or demos** for your carpenters?

## 5. Run a structured trial

1. **Pick one product line** and one press.
2. Produce a **defined batch** (for example 20–50 panels) with the new adhesive.
3. Record spread, open time, press time and any handling issues.
4. **Test samples:** break tests dry and after a water soak; check for bubbles and edge lift.
5. Install a few units in a **real kitchen** and monitor for several weeks.
6. Compare **kg per sheet** and any rework.

## 6. Switch in phases

Once the trial passes, roll out line by line. Keep both adhesives clearly labelled during transition, train carpenters on any differences in open time, and update your internal spec sheets.

## Red flags when buying adhesive

- No technical data sheet or durability class claim without test basis.
- Big batch-to-batch variation in viscosity or colour.
- "Waterproof" claims without specifying D3 or D4.
- No one to call when a problem occurs.

## Key takeaways

- Match **every product line to a D-class**; use **D3 for wet zones**.
- Compare **cost per sq ft and callback risk**, not price per kg.
- Choose a supplier with **in-house resin, documentation and technical support**.
- **Trial properly, then switch in phases.**
