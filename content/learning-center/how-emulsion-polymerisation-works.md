Almost every product Enlace makes — paint binders, construction resins, textile binders, coir emulsions and the resin inside Enfixx wood adhesives — is born the same way: through **emulsion polymerisation**. Understanding the process explains why two "acrylic emulsions" with the same name can perform very differently.

## The ingredients

- **Monomers** — small molecules that link into long polymer chains. Examples: methyl methacrylate, butyl acrylate, styrene, vinyl acetate, acrylic acid.
- **Water** — the continuous phase (typically around half of the finished product).
- **Surfactants (emulsifiers)** — stabilise monomer droplets and polymer particles in water.
- **Protective colloids** — such as polyvinyl alcohol in wood adhesives, for stability and tack.
- **Initiator** — generates free radicals that start polymerisation (for example persulphates or redox systems).
- **Buffers, chain-transfer agents and functional monomers** — control pH, molecular weight and special properties such as crosslinking.

## The process in five stages

1. **Pre-emulsion:** monomers, water and surfactant are mixed into a stable monomer emulsion.
2. **Seed/initiation:** part of the charge is heated in the reactor with initiator. Free radicals form and small **polymer particles** nucleate.
3. **Growth (feed stage):** the monomer emulsion and initiator are fed gradually. Monomer migrates into growing particles, where it polymerises. Feed rates and temperature are tightly controlled because polymerisation releases heat.
4. **Chase/post-treatment:** extra initiator reduces residual free monomer.
5. **Cooling and finishing:** pH is adjusted, additives such as biocides and defoamers are added, and the emulsion is filtered and packed.

## What the chemist controls

| Variable | Affects |
|---|---|
| Monomer ratio | Tg, hardness, flexibility, UV and water resistance |
| Particle size | Binding power, gloss, penetration, viscosity |
| Surfactant type & level | Stability, water sensitivity, foam |
| Molecular weight | Strength, toughness, tack, creep |
| Functional monomers | Crosslinking, adhesion, alkali resistance |
| Process temperature & feed profile | Particle morphology, residual monomer, consistency |

Small changes here produce big changes in performance — which is why **in-house polymerisation** matters for consistency. See [MFFT and Tg explained](/learning-center/mfft-and-tg-emulsion-polymers-explained).

## Quality control

A well-run emulsion plant tests every batch for:

- **Solids content**
- **Viscosity**
- **pH**
- **Particle size**
- **MFFT** (where relevant)
- **Grit/coagulum** (filterability)
- **Residual monomer**
- **Application tests** (film, adhesion, bond strength)

Retain samples are stored for traceability.

## Why it matters to buyers

Two suppliers can sell a "styrene acrylic emulsion with 50% solids" that behave differently because of particle size, surfactant, molecular weight and process control. Ask suppliers about **how** they make their products, not only the headline numbers. Our [supplier evaluation checklist](/learning-center/how-to-evaluate-new-emulsion-supplier) helps.

## Key takeaways

- Emulsion polymerisation turns **monomers into polymer particles dispersed in water**.
- Monomer choice, particle size, surfactants and process control decide **performance**.
- **In-house manufacturing and batch QC** are the foundations of consistency.
