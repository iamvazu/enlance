Two numbers appear on almost every emulsion technical data sheet: **MFFT** and **Tg**. They look academic, but they decide whether a paint forms a proper film on a cold morning, whether a wall picks up dirt in summer, and how much coalescent a formulator must add. Here's what they mean in practice.

## Glass transition temperature (Tg)

Every polymer has a **glass transition temperature**. Below Tg, polymer chains are frozen — the material is **hard and glassy**. Above Tg, chains can move — the material is **soft and rubbery**.

In emulsion polymers, Tg is set mainly by the **monomer mix**:

- **"Hard" monomers** (methyl methacrylate, styrene) raise Tg.
- **"Soft" monomers** (butyl acrylate, 2-ethylhexyl acrylate) lower Tg.

The Fox equation gives a first estimate of a copolymer's Tg from its monomer weight fractions, and chemists tune the ratio to hit the target.

## Minimum film forming temperature (MFFT)

An emulsion is billions of separate polymer particles in water. When it dries, those particles must **deform and fuse (coalesce)** into a continuous film. **MFFT** is the lowest temperature at which this happens. Below MFFT, you get a **white, powdery or cracked** film instead of a clear, continuous one.

MFFT is usually close to Tg but is shifted by particle size, surfactants, water plasticisation and additives.

## Why they matter for paints

| If the binder is… | Result |
|---|---|
| **Too soft** (low Tg/MFFT) | Easy film formation, flexible, low coalescent need — but **dirt pickup, blocking** (surfaces sticking together) and poor scrub resistance |
| **Too hard** (high Tg/MFFT) | Hard, clean, block-resistant film — but needs **coalescent** to form a film, and may crack on flexible substrates |
| **Balanced** | Good film formation at site temperatures, adequate hardness, acceptable coalescent level |

In India, application temperatures are usually warm, but early-morning and hill-station painting, cold-season work and air-conditioned interiors still require an MFFT low enough — or enough coalescent — for proper film formation.

## Coalescing agents

A **coalescing agent** is a slow-evaporating solvent that temporarily lowers MFFT so harder binders can form a film, then leaves to let the film harden. Trade-offs:

- More coalescent = more **VOC** and odour.
- Too little = **poor film formation**, reduced scrub and water resistance.

Modern low-VOC formulations aim for binders that form films with minimal coalescent while staying hard enough to resist dirt — often using **core-shell** or **self-crosslinking** technologies.

## Why they matter for adhesives

In **wood adhesives**, MFFT decides the lowest workshop temperature at which the glue line forms properly — the "chalk temperature". Below it, joints can be weak and chalky. Tg influences **creep** (joints slowly moving under load) and heat resistance. That's part of why different [D-class wood adhesives](/learning-center/d1-d2-d3-d4-wood-adhesive-classes-en-204-explained) behave differently.

## Why they matter for textile binders

For [pigment printing binders](/learning-center/textile-pigment-printing-binder-guide), lower Tg gives a **softer hand**, while higher Tg improves **rub fastness** and reduces tackiness. Formulators balance the two.

## Quick reference

- **Tg** = where the polymer turns from glassy to rubbery.
- **MFFT** = lowest temperature for a continuous film.
- **Low Tg** → soft, flexible, tacky; **high Tg** → hard, clean, may need coalescent.
- Always check MFFT against the **coldest realistic application temperature**.
